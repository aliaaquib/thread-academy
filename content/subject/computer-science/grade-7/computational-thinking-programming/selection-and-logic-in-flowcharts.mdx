Most programs cannot do the same thing every time — they have to react. A game reacts to your score, a login reacts to your password. That reacting has a name: selection.

<Definition term="Selection">
Choosing which instructions to run based on whether a condition is true or false.
</Definition>

## Key ideas

### The diamond does the choosing

In a flowchart, selection is the diamond: one arrow in, two arrows out — Yes and No. The program checks the condition, then follows the matching path. Everything after the two paths rejoin continues as normal.

### Decisions can sit inside decisions

A Yes path can lead straight into another diamond. Going out: "is it raining?" → Yes → "do you have an umbrella?" → Yes → "take it" / No → "stay in". Each extra diamond handles a more specific situation. This is called nesting, and it is just decisions inside decisions.

### Combining conditions: AND, OR, NOT

Sometimes one question is not enough. Three little words combine conditions:

- **AND** — both must be true. "age ≥ 12 AND has a ticket": a 14-year-old *with* a ticket gets in; a 14-year-old *without* one does not.
- **OR** — at least one must be true. "is Saturday OR is Sunday": either day means it is the weekend.
- **NOT** — flips the answer. "NOT raining" is true exactly when it is dry.

**Key term — condition:** A check that is either true or false, such as "score ≥ 50". Decisions test conditions.

### A real example: the purple test

Conditions are often comparisons of text:

<CodeBlock lang="Flowchart steps">
START → INPUT colour → ◆ colour == "purple"? → Yes → OUTPUT "I like purple too" → STOP
                                                      → No → OUTPUT "That colour is ok, but purple is better" → STOP
</CodeBlock>

Input "purple" takes the Yes arrow; input "green" takes the No arrow. Notice the condition, the true-action and the false-action are all visible — a selection always has all three.

### Boolean values come in many spellings

A condition's answer is a **Boolean**: True or False. You will also see it written as yes/no or 1/0 — same idea. When *both* conditions in an AND are False, the whole thing is False; when *one or both* are False, OR can still be True; NOT simply reverses the value.

<WorkedExample title="Trace the entry check">
The flowchart above runs for a visitor who is 10 years old and has a ticket.

Check the condition: "age ≥ 12" is false (10 is less than 12), "has ticket" is true. AND needs *both* — false AND true gives false. Take the No path: "Refuse entry".

**Answer:** Entry refused, even though they have a ticket — AND is strict. If the visitor were 14 with a ticket, both parts would be true and they would get in.
</WorkedExample>

<ImportantNote title="Common mistakes">
- **A diamond with only one path out** Correction: a decision must have both Yes and No paths leading somewhere — even if one just goes straight to STOP.
- **Mixing up AND and OR** Correction: AND needs both conditions true; OR needs only one. "Weekend" is Saturday OR Sunday, not AND — no day is both.
- **Reading NOT backwards** Correction: NOT flips the result. "NOT (score ≥ 50)" is true when the score is *below* 50.
</ImportantNote>

<Summary>
- Selection: choosing which instructions run based on whether a condition is true or false — the diamond in a flowchart.
- Decisions can nest: a Yes/No path can lead to another diamond for more specific situations.
- AND needs both conditions true; OR needs at least one; NOT flips a condition's answer.
- Watch out for: diamonds with a missing branch, mixing up AND and OR, and reading NOT backwards.
</Summary>
